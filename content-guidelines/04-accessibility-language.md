# Accessibility Language

Accessibility accuracy has higher priority than SEO, conversion and style. If a phrase sounds persuasive but could mislead a reader about suitability, rewrite it.

## Accessibility Is Individual

Never assume that two people using wheelchairs need the same facilities. Never describe accessibility as a simple yes/no attribute unless verified and contextually appropriate.

Prefer:

> "This accommodation offers a range of accessibility features."

Rather than:

> "This accommodation is completely accessible."

## Never Infer Accessibility

Gemma must never invent:

- Roll-in showers.
- Wet rooms.
- Hoists.
- Profiling beds.
- Grab rails.
- Accessible coaches.
- Accessible transfers.
- Step-free access.
- Carers.
- Airport assistance.
- Adapted excursions.
- Equipment hire.
- Room dimensions.
- Door widths.
- Pool hoists.
- Mobility equipment storage.

Only include these details when verified data explicitly supports the claim.

## Preferred Terminology

Use respectful, practical language:

- "Disabled people" or "people with disabilities", depending on sentence context.
- "Wheelchair users."
- "People who use a wheelchair."
- "Mobility scooter users."
- "People with reduced mobility."
- "Accessibility requirements."
- "Care requirements."
- "Support requirements."
- "Additional assistance."
- "Step-free access."
- "Accessible room" only when this is the verified product term, and still explain what features are included.

Some search phrases such as "disabled holidays" may be used because users actively search for them. Use them naturally and sparingly. Do not repeat SEO terminology in a way that sounds unnatural or reduces the person to a label.

## Language To Avoid

Avoid:

- "The disabled" as a noun.
- "Wheelchair-bound."
- "Confined to a wheelchair."
- "Suffers from."
- "Victim of."
- "Handicapped" except when quoting official terminology in a specific country.
- "Special needs" unless context absolutely requires it.
- "Normal travellers."
- "Despite their disability, they can enjoy..."
- "Fully accessible" unless the scope is precisely defined and verified.

## Suitability Wording

Avoid guarantees. Use this pattern:

Feature -> practical implication -> individual variation -> invitation to discuss requirements.

Example:

> "The hotel has step-free routes through several key areas. Whether the layout works for you will depend on your individual mobility requirements, so speak with our team if you'd like us to talk through the details."

## Claim Strength Rules

Use confident wording only for verified facts:

> "The room includes a wet room."

Use careful wording for general guidance:

> "A wet room can be useful if you need level-access showering, but the right setup depends on space, grab rail position and transfer requirements."

Use uncertainty wording where information is missing:

> "If a wet room is essential, ask our team to check availability for your dates before you book."

## Substantial Examples

| Situation | Use | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| General destination | "Some areas may be easier to navigate than others, so consider gradients, pavements, transport and distances." | "The destination is wheelchair friendly." |
| Hotel access | "Ask whether step-free routes are available between the entrance, reception, restaurant and your room." | "The hotel is accessible throughout." |
| Room features | "Check the exact bathroom layout, shower access and transfer space before deciding." | "Accessible rooms have everything you need." |
| Mobility scooter | "Confirm scooter transport, storage and charging arrangements in advance." | "You can take your scooter anywhere." |
| Excursions | "Some excursions may involve steps, uneven ground or longer distances." | "All excursions are suitable." |
| Care support | "If you need care or support during your holiday, discuss what can be arranged before booking." | "Care is included." |
| Airport | "Airport assistance usually needs to be requested in advance and may vary by airport and airline." | "Airport staff will handle everything." |
| Coach travel | "Ask whether the coach, boarding process and seating arrangement will work for your mobility requirements." | "The coach is wheelchair accessible." |
| Cruise | "Cabin layout, lift access, gangways and excursions can all affect suitability." | "Cruises are ideal for disabled travellers." |
| Beach access | "Beach access can depend on surfaces, gradients and available equipment." | "The beach is accessible." |

## Accessibility Caveat Pattern

Use caveats without making the article feel evasive.

Good:

> "This can be a good option for some wheelchair users, but suitability depends on the room layout, bathroom setup and transfer arrangements."

Too vague:

> "It depends."

Too strong:

> "This is suitable for wheelchair users."

## When To Ask For A Phone Conversation

Invite a phone conversation when:

- The reader needs personalised suitability advice.
- Accessibility features must be checked for specific dates.
- The article discusses equipment, care, transfers or room layout.
- The search query has commercial or destination intent.
- Missing information could materially affect the holiday.

The phone CTA should be helpful:

> "If any of these details are essential for your trip, speak with our team. We can talk through your requirements and help check what is available."
